01
Why quantum?

Two reasons ordinary computers are running out of room

Why quantum computing?

Limit 1: chips

Transistors are now only a few dozen atoms wide. Any smaller and quantum effects break the circuit.

Limit 2: nature

To imitate a quantum system, every extra particle doubles the numbers a normal computer must store.

Rice on a chessboard

Each square doubles the one before. A quantum system does the same with every extra particle.

“…if you want to make a simulation of nature, you'd better make it quantum mechanical.”

—Richard Feynman (lecture 1981, published 1982)

The takeaway

A specialist for certain problems, not a faster laptop.

Next: how one qubit works.

02
One qubit

What a qubit is, and what happens when you read it

Bit vs qubit

A bit is a switch. A qubit is an arrow that can point anywhere on a sphere.

Each part of the mix has a weight. The bigger the weight, the more likely you read that value.

Physicists write the two basic states as |0⟩ and |1⟩. The brackets are just name tags.

Bit and qubit, side by side

BitQubit
What it holds0 or 10, 1 or a mix of both
Reading itgives the stored valuegives 0 or 1 at random
After readingunchangedchanged for good
Copyingeasyimpossible

0 and 1 at the same time

Superposition: a qubit holding 0 and 1 at once, each with its own weight.

Weights can be positive or negative. Ordinary chances can't be. This is what makes quantum different.

Plus and minus both read as 50% 0 and 50% 1, yet they are different states.

Two common myths

Picturing a qubit

The Bloch sphere. Quantum gates simply rotate this arrow (coming up). It works for one qubit only.

  • North pole = 0, south pole = 1, equator = the 50/50 mixes
  • Height sets the chance of reading 0 or 1
  • Direction around is the phase: a plain reading can't see it, but it changes what happens next
  • Plus and minus: same height, opposite direction
The math of one qubit

A qubit: |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ with |α|² + |β|² = 1. Reading gives 0 with chance |α|² and 1 with chance |β|².

plus = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2, minus = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2. On the sphere: |ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + eiφ sin(θ/2)|1⟩, with θ the height and φ the phase.

Source: Nielsen & Chuang (2010), Fig. 1.3.

The hidden direction

Reading: a one-way door

After reading, the arrow snaps to a pole and everything else is lost.

Random: even if you know the qubit exactly, you only know the odds, never the single result.

Collapse: after reading, the qubit becomes the answer. The mix is gone for good.

A huge store, a tiny door

What our run showed

Our run: 1,024 runs per state, on an ideal noise-free simulator.

51% / 49%

for both plus and minus: they look identical

100%

zeros for state 0, every time

That is why quantum programs are run thousands of times.

Weights act like waves

Weights can cancel

Equal signs add up, opposite signs cancel.

Add one extra step (a gate called H) before reading. Each result can then be reached along two paths.

100%

Our run, 1,024 runs: plus always gives 0, minus always gives 1

The key idea

Ordinary chances only add up. Quantum weights can cancel.

Algorithms boost the right answer and cancel the wrong ones.

How qubits combine, and what they can never do

From one qubit to many

This is the doubling from the start. Entanglement is a big reason quantum computers are so hard to imitate.

Independent qubits: each can be described on its own. Easy for a normal computer.

Entangled qubits: only the whole group can be described. All 2n weights are needed.

Two gloves in two boxes?

Bell's game

Bell's game, also called the CHSH game.

Alice and Bob each get question A or B at random and answer 0 or 1, with no talking.

They win if the answers match, unless both got B: then the answers must differ.

The most quantum physics allows

Bell test result

Entanglement is not answers fixed in advance. It is a genuinely new resource.

Confirmed by real experiments: Nobel Prize 2022 (Aspect, Clauser, Zeilinger).

Math: many qubits, Bell

The numbers behind the last few slides:

  • n qubits: 2n complex weights; independent qubits need only 2 per qubit
  • Bell pair: (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2
  • Bell's game: best fixed plan 75%; entangled pair cos²(π/8) ≈ 85.4%, the most quantum physics allows

You cannot copy a qubit

Reading first doesn't help: one reading gives one bit and destroys the mix.

Rule: no machine can copy an unknown qubit.

A machine that copies 0 and 1 correctly turns a mix into a linked pair, not two copies.

Bits are fine, which is why ordinary data can be copied freely.

Math: why copying fails

A copier that maps |0⟩|0⟩ → |0⟩|0⟩ and |1⟩|0⟩ → |1⟩|1⟩ must, by linearity, send α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ to α|00⟩ + β|11⟩: an entangled pair (circuit on the next slide).

A true copy would be (α|0⟩ + β|1⟩)(α|0⟩ + β|1⟩), a different state. So no such copier can exist.

Source: Nielsen & Chuang (2010), Box 12.1; Wootters & Zurek (1982).

04
Quantum gates

How do qubits change without losing information?

Rule 1: gates can be undone

A classical AND gate loses information: an output of 0 could come from 00, 01 or 10, so you can't go back.

A quantum gate never does: n qubits in, n qubits out, and you can always run it backwards. The one step you can't undo is measurement.

In math terms, gates are “unitary” matrices (more on slide 43).

Flip the bit, flip the sign

X gate

Quantum NOT: swaps |0⟩ and |1⟩.

Z gate

Sign flip: |1⟩ gets a minus sign, |0⟩ is untouched.

Y gate

Does both at once: flips the bit and the sign.

The minus sign is invisible at measurement, but it decides what cancels later.

Hadamard: the coin flip

H moves |0⟩ from the north pole to the equator: a 50/50 superposition.

50 / 50

H turns a certain |0⟩ into an equal superposition

H, then H

brings |0⟩ back for certain (a real coin flipped twice stays random!)

Almost every algorithm starts with H on every qubit: all inputs at once, equal weight.

CNOT links two qubits

If the first qubit is 1, flip the second.

InputOutputWhat happens
0000First is 0: nothing happens
0101First is 0: nothing happens
1011First is 1: flip the second
1110First is 1: flip the second

Give it a 50/50 first qubit and the pair is linked: (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2. Measure one, and you know the other.

The catch

Can build anything is not the same as fast

The math behind gates

Source: Nielsen & Chuang (2010), Fig. 4.2.

  • Gates are unitary: U†U = I, so probability is preserved and U−1 = U†
  • CNOT: |c, t⟩ → |c, t ⊕ c⟩
  • {H, S, T, CNOT} approximates any operation to any accuracy (S = T²)
  • Solovay–Kitaev: single-qubit gates need only O(logc(1/ε)) gates, c ≈ 2; arbitrary n-qubit operations can still need exponentially many

Grover's search

N items, no order, no index, one is special. Find it.

Steps needed as the list grows (log-log). Classical N/2, Grover about 0.8 × √N.

Each round: mark, then amplify

Amplify (diffusion)Flip every amplitude around the average: the marked one jumps up, the rest shrink.

The Grover formulas

The numbers behind the search:

  • Start angle θ with sin θ = 1/√N
  • Each round turns the state by 2θ
  • Diffusion reflects about the mean: a → 2·mean − a
  • 3 qubits, amplitude of 101: 0.354 → 0.884 → 0.972
  • No quantum algorithm beats order √N (Bennett et al., 1997)

Quantum machine learning

Approach 1
Quantum kernels (QSVM)
  • Encode data x as a quantum state |φ(x)⟩
  • The quantum chip estimates similarity K(x, x′) = |⟨φ(x)|φ(x′)⟩|²
  • A classical SVM does the rest (Havlíček et al., Nature 2019)

We ran one: results coming up

Approach 2
Variational circuits (VQC) runs today
  • Gates have trainable angles θ, like network weights
  • The quantum chip runs the circuit; a classical optimizer updates θ
  • Shallow circuits make it practical on NISQ devices

Try it: Qiskit Machine Learning, PennyLane

The hybrid loop

Data x → Circuit U(θ) → Measure → Classical optimizer → Update θ, repeat

HHL: exponential speedup, on paper

s = sparsity · κ = condition number · ε = precision

Classical · conjugate gradient

O(N · s · κ)

Quantum · HHL, 2009

O(log N · s²κ² / ε)

Four conditions attached

  • A must be sparse and well-conditioned (small κ)
  • The vector b must load into a quantum state |b⟩ quickly
  • The output is a state |x⟩, not the vector: reading all N entries costs O(N)
  • Useful only when you need a summary value such as ⟨x|M|x⟩

Quantum optimization

The problem
Many data tasks are QUBO problems

Feature selection, clustering, scheduling and routing all explode combinatorially.

Gate-based
QAOA (Farhi et al., 2014)

A hybrid, shallow-circuit algorithm designed for today's noisy hardware.

Special-purpose
Quantum annealing (D-Wave)

Special-purpose hardware with thousands of qubits, but not a general-purpose computer.

Reality check: no clear advantage yet over the best classical heuristics on real-world problems.

The catch

Every speedup so far assumes the data is already inside the quantum computer.

So how does big data get in?

Bottleneck 1: loading the data

Quantum addressing for a 32-cell memory. Source: Nielsen & Chuang (2010), Fig. 6.9.

  • Big data lives on disks as classical bits, while the algorithms assume it is already a quantum state.
  • That needs QRAM: memory addressed by an index held in superposition.
  • The textbook design uses O(N log N) quantum switches, roughly the hardware of the database itself.
  • Loading N records alone costs about O(N), erasing a √N or log N speedup. No large, noise-tolerant QRAM exists yet.
Nielsen & Chuang conclude that Grover's main use is likely solving hard search problems such as SAT or TSP, not searching classical databases.

—Nielsen & Chuang (2010), §6.5

Three takeaways

A co-processor, not a replacement

Quantum computers win only on narrow problem classes: simulation, factoring, some search and optimization.

The bottleneck is data I/O

For big data, the hard part is getting data in and out of the machine, not computing fast.

Always ask “compared to what?”

Follow post-quantum cryptography, try hybrid QML on a simulator, and read the assumptions behind any speedup claim.
